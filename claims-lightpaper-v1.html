Abstract. Three hundred million scientific papers contain roughly a trillion words of expert knowledge — the substrate on which roughly three trillion dollars of annual research and development depends. Almost none of it is structured as machine-readable and AI-actionable data. The current generation of AI tools cannot fix this: frontier large language models fabricate scientific references at rates above sixty per cent on long-form question answering1,2. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems eliminate fabrication. Our own product SciWeave, for instance, hits a 0% hallucinated-reference rate on the ScholarQABench benchmark1,3. But RAG systems can only return summary paragraphs. They cannot see how solid the evidence for a claim is or where the literature has gaps and missing links that enable scientific progress. Claims is a Bittensor subnet that produces the missing layer: a structured graph of subject–predicate–object claims, each linked to the specific evidence in the source paper that supports, contradicts, or qualifies it. Miners extract claim–evidence pairs from peer-reviewed papers selected by validators; validators score miners against reference models and gold datasets curated with domain experts and assemble the canonical graph for science. The resulting structured data is a propietary, off-chain product accessible via metered API and MCP connections. Controlled-vocabulary mappings and customer-specific ontologies enhance the data and enable model training and AI research workflows for pharma, biotech, and defense R&D clients. Product revenue is partly recycled into the subnet Alpha pool through auto-staking, which anchors token value to usage.

1. Introduction

Roughly three trillion U.S. dollars are spent globally each year on research and development4. Over 300 million scientific works and adjacent technical artefacts exist containing more than a trillion words, all optimized for human consumption. None of this corpus is natively structured in a way that machines can make sense of it. Every scientist, every R&D team, every regulator, every scientific AI system that needs to know "what is currently known about X, with what evidence, and what contradicts it" reconstructs that knowledge independently by hand or by approximation.

Generative AI could potentially outperform leading human scientists and speed up scientific progress exponentially. However, the current reality looks different. Frontier large language models, queried without retrieval tools, fabricate cited papers at rates above sixty per cent on long-form scientific question-answering tasks1,2,5. This disqualifies them for serious research work. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) — the architecture adopted by every credible scientific AI assistant — solves the hallucination problem by constraining the model to passages it actually retrieves3,6,7. We have shipped such a RAG architecture at production scale: SciWeave, the consumer product built by our team, reaches a 0% hallucinated-reference rate on the citation-grounded ScholarQABench benchmark introduced by Asai et al. in Nature1,3. But RAG systems can only paraphrase what authors wrote. They cannot reliably spot disagreements or gaps, differentiate between weak and strong evidence, identify replication problems, or surface plausible links that have not been explored yet.

The remaining gap is structural, not scale. No amount of training compute alone characterises the negative space of scientific knowledge because a generative model can only retrieve and recombine what authors have already said. To make that space computable, the literature has to be decomposed into machine-readable claims, each linked to its specific evidence, with provenance, evidence type, quantitative detail, and explicit relationships (supports / contradicts / refines / replicates) between claims. That is the substrate we propose to build.

We argue here that producing this substrate is a task that belongs on Bittensor rather than inside any single company for three reasons. First, claim extraction from a fixed paper is probabilistic: any one model run is a point estimate, and consensus across many independently configured extractors yields both a sharper signal and a calibrated confidence interval per claim. A single-vendor pipeline collapses extraction onto one model's view. Second, the resulting commodity has objectively measurable quality dimensions that an incentive mechanism can reward (e.g. accuracy and granularity evaluated against expert-curated gold sets and reference models). An open competition over those dimensions raises data quality faster than any single-vendor pipeline can match. Third, token-funded distributed labour is structurally more capital-efficient than payroll-funded extraction at the scale of the full scientific corpus.

2. Background

2.1 Hallucinated citations are a present problem, not a future risk

Wilder & Walters5 showed in Scientific Reports that ChatGPT fabricates bibliographic citations at high rates and that even its real GPT-4 citations contain substantive errors in roughly a quarter of cases. Recent domain-specific replications confirm the pattern. Manley et al.2 asked GPT-4o to write a plastic-surgery literature review with citations: of 21 references generated, 5 were correct, 8 had publication-detail errors, and 8 could not be located on PubMed at all — a fabrication rate of 38%. Glynn8 documents fabricated citations already entering peer-reviewed bibliographies; downstream papers then cite the contaminated manuscripts, and the error compounds at the corpus level. Pellegrina & Helmy9 review current AI-error detection tools and find them insufficient to plug the gap at manuscript-submission volumes.

Asai et al.1, publishing in Nature, introduced ScholarQABench — a citation-grounded long-form scientific QA benchmark covering 1,451 biomedical and 1,308 neuroscience questions — precisely to make these failure modes measurable. On a 100-question random sample we ran for SciWeave's benchmark report3, GPT-5.2 hallucinated 59% of its cited papers and Claude Opus 4.6 hallucinated 62%.

2.2 What RAG solves, and what it does not

Retrieval-augmented generation constrains an LLM to passages retrieved from an external index. When the index is curated to peer-reviewed sources, fabrication is structurally precluded: any citation the model emits is a real document, by construction6,7. SciWeave's retrieval index spans roughly 300 million scientific works via OpenAlex, ClinicalTrials.gov, Cochrane, and PubMed. On 100 sample question from ScholarQABench and thousands of referenced articles (~10 per query), SciWeave hallucinated zero papers, while also having higher citation recall and precission than frontier LLMs.3.

However, solving the hallucinated references problem is not sufficient to enable precise scientific reasoning by AI. Additional recent benchmark studies make this concrete. Li et al.10, in Science Advances, evaluate retrieval-augmented LLMs across five biomedical natural-language-processing tasks and eleven datasets, including counterfactual and self-awareness testbeds, and report that RAG systems still struggle with robustness when retrieved passages are noisy, distractive, or counterfactual — and remain weak at recognising when they should abstain. Hou et al.11, evaluating a dietary-supplement RAG system, find that pairing retrieval with a structured knowledge graph lifts accuracy from 62% (bare GPT-4o) to 99% (RAG-over-graph) on a 1,000-question evaluation set. Joy & Su12 show a similar pattern on biomedical mechanism questions: federated retrieval over more than sixty curated knowledge sources raises GPT-4o gene-mechanism accuracy from 51% to 75.8%.

The pattern across these results is consistent: vanilla RAG removes fabrication but caps short of the accuracy required for high-stakes scientific reasoning. The next frontier requires structured data in combination with retrieval (e.g. knowledge graphs, controlled vocabularies, and claim-level evidence linkage). Such a canonical knowledge graph for science does not exist yet. That is the gap Claims fills.

2.3 The state of automated claim–evidence extraction

The closest published work to what miners on Claims will produce is the line on automated scientific claim verification and structured extraction. Edelman & Skolnick13 describe Valsci, an open-source utility that combines retrieval, bibliometric scoring, and chain-of-thought prompting to verify scientific claims at batch scale against Semantic Scholar; on the SciFact benchmark, Valsci substantially reduces citation hallucination over base GPT-4o while maintaining low misclassification.

What this literature does not yet produce is a continuously maintained, openly accessible, decision-grade graph: claims as atomic subject–predicate–object triples; evidence records with study design, sample size, effect size, confidence interval, and boundary conditions; provenance to the originating paper and passage; explicit support / contradict / refine relationships between claims. Existing tools either evaluate isolated claims, generate summaries, or remain inside the walled garden of one application. The shared substrate is missing. The remainder of this paper specifies one and proposes a market mechanism to produce it.

3. The digital commodity

Miners on the Claims subnet produce a small, stable set of structured objects extracted from peer-reviewed scientific papers: a Paper (source bibliographic metadata), one or more Spans (precise text passages from the paper that anchor a claim or evidence for provenance), one or more Claims (each a subject–predicate–object proposition with epistemic metadata), one or more EvidenceItems (the empirical or theoretical results that bear on a claim), and explicit ClaimEvidenceLinks relating claims to the evidence that supports, qualifies, or contradicts them. Together these five objects form the queryable graph. The full prototype schema is reproduced in Appendix 1 and tracked on the public RFC repository25; the conceptual overview and a worked example follow.

The fields below are a conceptual overview. The complete specification (object schemas as JSON, profile definitions, ontology-annotation pattern, and graph translation) is in Appendix 1.

Two stable extension rules. The schema deliberately keeps the object model fixed and pushes variability into two places: context (applicability qualifiers — population, species, dose, comparator, timepoint, assay type) and details (type-specific structured payloads — for example, an RCT EvidenceItem's details exposes effect_size, ci_low, ci_high, p_value, sample_size, while a laboratory-experiment EvidenceItem's details exposes measurement_type, target, assay_type, and so on). Domain-specific shapes are codified as profiles rather than as new top-level objects.

Ontology enhancement. Any field whose value benefits from semantic normalisation — a drug, a gene, a disease, a species, a method, a predicate — is wrapped in a uniform SemanticField structure that carries the raw text, a normalised text, candidate ontology mappings, a selected mapping, and the mapping method. The same pattern is reused everywhere ontology enhancement is relevant. Mapping is optional: a claim with no resolved mapping is still queryable on its raw text, and mapping adds semantic recall and disambiguation. The choice of ontology vocabularies — MeSH, ChEBI, NCBITaxon, ECO, customer-specific controlled vocabularies — is per-deployment and is the substrate on which the Palantir-Foundry-style customer-ontology moat (§4.3) is built.

Why claims, not papers. A paper is a unit of authorship; a claim is a unit of knowledge. A single paper contains many claims of varying strength; many papers make overlapping claims with differing evidence. Modeling claims as first-class objects — rather than treating the paper as the atomic unit — is the design choice that makes the negative space computable and that supports the structured disagreement layer described in §4.3.

The data product. The miner-produced stream of structured ClaimRecords is the substrate on which the company builds. Public, openly accessible canonical claim handles exist only at the level required for cross-validation and audit. The full, enriched graph — with controlled-vocabulary mappings, customer-specific ontologies, contradiction maps, evidence-strength scoring, and curated Views — is paywalled under a freemium model, with tailor-made solutions for enterprise customers on top. We currently have one paid pilot project with a biotech startup in the making, with two additional potential clients (biotech & defense) lined up after we have shipped the first pilot.

4. Architecture and roles

4.1 System overview

4.2 Miners

A miner is any party that registers on the subnet and runs an extraction pipeline — typically a configured large language model with retrieval, parsing, and entity-normalisation stages, plus any custom logic the miner has developed. Miners do not select what to work on. For each task, a miner receives a task envelope containing:

  • the source paper's DOI and accessible text (abstract always, full text when openly accessible),
  • the task family (claim extraction, evidence linking, entity normalisation, or supplementary metadata),
  • the schema version against which output will be scored.

The miner returns a batch of ClaimRecords conforming to the schema. The miner's identity is not consulted: scoring is over the structured output, not over reputation, narrative, or any off-chain signal.

Hardware and skill floor. Extraction compute is relatively cheap. For example, open-source LLMs on Chutes can run a paper for cents, and the full literature is single-digit millions of dollars of inference at current prices. The skill floor is given by a sample extraction pipeline that we will share with the miner community on our GitHub repository. The intentional consequence is a low entrance barrier, with higher earning potential for miners with high engineering quality.

4.3 Validators

A validator is a party with sufficient stake to be eligible to score miners. Validators perform four bound tasks:

  1. Task selection. Validators choose which papers enter the extraction queue. Choices are not justified or disclosed at the protocol layer. In practice this is the channel through which downstream value enters the subnet: validators preferentially queue recent papers, high-citation papers, papers in domains a paying customer needs, papers triggered by an on-demand query.
  2. Cohort assignment with hidden overlap. For each selected paper, the validator dispatches the extraction task to a random subset of registered miners — for example, a cohort of 10 out of a larger active set. Which miners share a paper is not disclosed to the miners and is reshuffled on a per-paper basis. This is load-bearing: miners cannot identify their direct competitors on any given paper, which removes the equilibrium where a few miners coordinate or copy one another. The subnet still benefits from cross-miner consensus signal because the validator can see the cohort's outputs together.
  3. Scoring against reference and gold. The validator scores each returned ClaimRecord against their reference model on dimensions that include precision, recall, granularity, evidence accuracy, and grounding. The exogenous anchor is a gold dataset: a stratified sample of papers for which human domain experts have manually produced the correct ClaimRecord set, using the same schema as miners. Gold-annotated papers are indistinguishable from normal work when injected into the task stream, and the pool is rotated to prevent memorisation. Gold curation is paid for from the validators' protocol-emission share (§6), with reviewer recruitment routed through the DeSci Labs AI Reviewer Finder16, which already matches manuscripts and topic areas to qualified scientific reviewers.
  4. Canonical graph curation. Validators merge accepted miner outputs into a canonical claim–evidence graph: cluster near-duplicate claims across miners by semantic similarity, select the highest-quality version of each, union the evidence records, and link the subject–predicate–object triples to controlled vocabularies and ontologies through on-demand Views. The choice of vocabulary depends on the validator's specialisation and the customer engagements they support. This step is where the Palantir Foundry analogy applies most directly: the ontology layer is co-developed with paying clients in pharma, defense, or economics, and the resulting customer-specific schemas are non-transferable and switching-cost-heavy.

Validator specialisation as a feature. Specialization among validators is expected and encouraged. For example, a pharma-focused validator will queue oncology and metabolic-disease papers; defense-focused validators will queue dual-use chemistry and material science; finance-focused validators will queue finance, micro-economics, statistics etc. Validators will also be responsible for curating their own gold-sets to anchor their evaluations. Validators can also develop and apply their own controlled vocabularies and ontologies in collaboration with their own clients, feeding the enriched data back into the Claims database. The shared substrate is the same; the surface area sold to customers differs. This is the structural reason multiple independent validators on Claims are commercially additive rather than redundant.

4.4 Customers

The demand side of Claims has three buyers:

  • R&D teams in pharma, biotech, defense, and other evidence-intensive sectors. Buy: a metered API and MCP server that returns structured ClaimRecords for arbitrary scientific questions; enterprise plans that include a customer-specific ontology layer and validation screens for the failure modes specific to the customer's R&D problem.
  • Scientific AI builders wiring grounded reasoning into agentic systems, scientific copilots, regulatory review tools, and literature-summarisation products. Buy: a higher-throughput API tier and tools to filter by evidence type, study design, and replication status.
  • Frontier AI labs training the next generation of scientific reasoning models. Buy: licensed access to the structured graph as high-quality, citation-grounded post-training and reinforcement-learning data.

SciWeave, the team's existing consumer product, is the primary distribution surface for the free and lower-priced tiers and the natural place to onboard researchers who later become enterprise prospects.

5. Incentive mechanism

The current state is summarised below as a brief orientation; the full mechanism design — scoring function, weight aggregation under Yuma, gold-set weighting, anti-gaming defences, and the owner-curator conflict-of-interest resolution — is in active development and will replace this placeholder in lightpaper v0.2.

Brief orientation for reviewers. Bittensor's Yuma consensus has no native ground-truth signal: validators submit weight vectors over miners, and miners are paid by the stake-weighted consensus of those weights. The mechanism-design challenge is to anchor miner rewards to actual claim-extraction quality without (a) collapsing onto a single reference LLM, (b) creating a free-rider problem among validators, or (c) requiring the subnet owner to hold a privileged information edge that compromises the open-substrate framing. The current design space combines a precision floor against rotating secret gold datasets, an optional held-out downstream benchmark, and an adversarial-claim-catch mechanism in which miners must distinguish genuine claims from deliberately-injected fakes. Formal specification by Christian is expected in v0.2. See the Validator-Incentive Design Brief (internal) for the full design constraints.

Value loop. High-quality data is the moat. Miner output enriches the canonical graph, which raises product quality, which raises usage, which raises revenue, which funds subnet Alpha token buyback, which attracts miners and validators. The on-chain token captures a measurable share of that product's economic surplus.

7. Evidence and traction

[NOTE TO TEAM — DELETE BEFORE EXTERNAL CIRCULATION]
Claims is pre-launch. The credibility of a pre-launch subnet rests on three substitutes: a prior shipped product with measurable performance; a theory-derived mechanism backed by named expertise; and grounded literature support for the design choices. We carry all three.

7.1 SciWeave: a shipped product, on a published benchmark

SciWeave is a production AI research assistant built by the same team. It is available as a web application (sciweave.com), iOS and Android apps, and an MCP server that plugs into Claude, ChatGPT, Codex, Cursor, Windsurf, and other AI clients. Its retrieval index spans approximately 300 million scientific works via OpenAlex19, plus ClinicalTrials.gov, Cochrane, PubMed, NIH Gene, and proteomics databases. Public partnerships with OpenAlex, CrossRef, and ORCID — the three core providers of modern academic infrastructure — are in production.

On the ScholarQABench1 citation-grounded long-form QA benchmark (100-question random sample, biomedical and neuroscience), SciWeave produced a 0% hallucinated-reference rate; GPT-5.2 hallucinated 59% and Claude Opus 4.6 hallucinated 62%. SciWeave also exceeded both on citation recall and precision while citing more papers per answer (~10 vs ~3.7 and ~7.1). Full methodology, exact commands, and replication scripts are public3,20.

7.2 First commercial pilot in motion

A privately-held drug-discovery startup is currently building an FDA-relevant decision workflow on top of structured claim-level evidence from Claims-style extractions. A prototype is in active development; first demo to the customer's leadership is pending. Two further enterprise prospects (in biotech and defense R&D) are gated on the first pilot's outcome.

The validator incentive mechanism is being developed by Prof. Christian Roessler, an economist specialising in game theory and mechanism design (see §8). His published work in Journal of Political Economy, European Economic Review, Economica, Economic Inquiry, and Journal of Mathematical Economics spans collective commitment, regulatory consistency, entrepreneurship and organisation design, and price-increasing competition. 7.4 Literature grounding

The design choices in this paper are anchored in the recent peer-reviewed literature on hallucination1,2,5,8,9, retrieval-augmented generation and its remaining gaps6,7,10,11,12, and automated claim extraction and verification13,14. The remaining ~50% replication-failure rate of published findings — documented across psychology21, biomedicine22, and the wider sciences23,24 — is itself the structural reason the evidence-strength dimension of Claims is necessary: even perfectly extracted claims need calibrated confidence intervals that reflect both extractor variance and source replication status.

7.5 RFC repository and V1 prototype

The prototype schema and its supporting documents are tracked on a public RFC repository25 and are open for review and contribution. A V1 prototype26 demonstrates the schema in production on three peer-reviewed papers: a Claims Viewer tab surfaces the extracted Paper, Span, Claim, EvidenceItem, and ClaimEvidenceLink objects, and a Graph Query Demos tab walks through Cypher queries against the resulting Neo4j graph — including evidence filtering by study design, ontology-mediated retrieval, and cross-paper queries that surface contradictions and gaps the underlying papers do not state individually. The prototype is small (three papers) but end-to-end, and demonstrates that the schema is implementable today, not aspirational.

The on-chain protocol does not warrant the copyright status of any paper a validator queues; participants bear their own compliance risk.
Owner-exit / rugOwner liquidates Alpha holdings, drains the AMM pool, abandons the subnet.Conviction governance upgrade makes owner exits public on-chain events; named accountable founding team; the off-chain commercial business is a larger value pool than any liquidation. Residual risk: not fully mitigated by mechanism alone.

10. Roadmap

Phase 1 — Centralised prototype (now → fall 2026). Build the customer-facing extraction-and-validation pipeline in-house. Sign the first paid R&D pilot. Prove that the prototype beats the customer's internal manual baseline on a head-to-head evidence-synthesis benchmark.

Phase 2 — Subnet launch (winter 2026). Register the Claims subnet on Bittensor. Migrate the extraction and validation layer to miners and independent validators. Operate the reference validator and miner. Open-source the miner reference implementation; keep the off-chain validator stack and the proprietary screening / ontology layers private. Onboard the first wave of independent miners and at least one independent validator.

Phase 3 — Platform (2027+). Expand beyond biomedicine to additional sciences. Onboard the second and third enterprise customers on customer-specific ontologies. Engage the protocol-level revenue buyback for subnet Alpha tokens.

11. Conclusion

Frontier AI fabricates scientific citations. RAG systems eliminate fabrication but do not produce the structured layer that high-stakes scientific decisions require. The next reliability axis is structure: claims as atomic objects, evidence as quantitative records, provenance as a first-class field, contradiction preserved rather than averaged away. Claims produces that substrate as the output of an open market in extraction quality on Bittensor, sells access through a freemium product layer modelled on the Palantir Foundry pattern, and routes revenue back into the Alpha pool through protocol-level buyback. The team built the consumer product that proved the underlying retrieval pipeline works. The mechanism-design lead is in place to specify the incentive layer that turns the open market into a quality-compounding substrate. The first commercial pilot is in motion.

Appendix 1 — Prototype Claim Graph Schema

This appendix reproduces the current prototype schema. It is preliminary and subject to revision; the canonical version lives in the public RFC repository25, and a V1 implementation is queryable at the prototype URL26.

A1.1 Design principles

Stable base objects. The prototype keeps a small fixed set of top-level objects: Paper, Span, Claim, EvidenceItem, ClaimEvidenceLink. Optional supporting objects: Entity, OntologyAnnotation.

Uniform ontology enhancement. Any field whose value benefits from semantic normalisation is wrapped in the same SemanticField structure, which carries a raw value, an entity type, and an OntologyAnnotation block (candidate mappings, selected mapping, mapping status, mapping method). The same shape is reused everywhere; there are no field-specific wrappers.

Extensibility in two places. The model grows through context (qualifiers and applicability conditions) and details (type-specific structured payloads). New domains are accommodated by profiles that define which keys are expected for a given evidence method or outcome type — no new top-level objects required. This prevents schema sprawl while still allowing rich domain coverage.

A1.2 Object model

A1.2.1 Paper

The source document or source artefact. Identifies the source and stores high-level bibliographic metadata; parent object for claims and spans.

{
  "paper_id": "paper_123",
  "doi": "10.1234/example",
  "pmid": "12345678",
  "title": "Example Paper",
  "authors": ["A. Smith", "B. Jones"],
  "journal": "Journal Name",
  "year": 2026,
  "source_type": "journal_article",
  "open_access": true
}

A1.2.2 Span

A precise source anchor from the paper. Anchors a claim or evidence item to exact source text; can also represent table, figure, or caption anchors; carries the provenance needed for export and verification.

{
  "span_id": "span_45",
  "paper_id": "paper_123",
  "section_type": "RESULTS",
  "section_name": "Primary outcome",
  "page": 7,
  "char_start": 18452,
  "char_end": 18527,
  "text": "Mean anxiety score decreased by 3.1 points (95% CI -5.4 to -0.8, p=0.01, n=184).",
  "table_id": null,
  "figure_id": null,
  "caption_id": null
}

A1.2.3 OntologyAnnotation

The uniform structure used inside any .ontology subfield. Stores ontology enhancement in one consistent shape; supports successful mappings and unresolved cases; can be used for concepts, qualifiers, and evidence-level classification.

{
  "annotation_type": "mapping",
  "raw_text": "5-HT1A",
  "normalized_text": "5-HT1A receptor",
  "mapping_status": "mapped",
  "candidate_mappings": [
    {
      "ontology_source": "CustomReceptorOntology",
      "ontology_id": "REC:5HT1A",
      "ontology_label": "5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 1A",
      "match_type": "exact",
      "confidence": 0.94
    }
  ],
  "selected_mapping": {
    "ontology_source": "CustomReceptorOntology",
    "ontology_id": "REC:5HT1A",
    "ontology_label": "5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 1A"
  },
  "mapping_method": "dictionary_plus_context"
}

Field meanings: annotation_type = mapping | classification; mapping_status = mapped | ambiguous | unresolved | rejected; candidate_mappings = ranked ontology candidates; selected_mapping = chosen concept if available.

A1.2.4 SemanticField

A reusable wrapper for any concept-like field. Keeps raw value and ontology enhancement separate; should be used wherever a field may need semantic normalisation. Recommended usage: Claim.subject, Claim.object, Claim.context.*, EvidenceItem.context.*, concept-like fields inside details.

{
  "value": "Psilocybin",
  "entity_type": "chemical",
  "ontology": {
    "annotation_type": "mapping",
    "raw_text": "Psilocybin",
    "normalized_text": "psilocybin",
    "mapping_status": "mapped",
    "candidate_mappings": [
      {
        "ontology_source": "ChEBI",
        "ontology_id": "CHEBI:0000",
        "ontology_label": "Psilocybin",
        "match_type": "exact",
        "confidence": 0.99
      }
    ],
    "selected_mapping": {
      "ontology_source": "ChEBI",
      "ontology_id": "CHEBI:0000",
      "ontology_label": "Psilocybin"
    },
    "mapping_method": "dictionary_plus_context"
  }
}

A1.2.5 Claim

A paper-specific assertion with a queryable subject–predicate–object core plus epistemic metadata. Contains the main proposition the system should reason over; keeps SPO explicit but not as the entire data model; uses context for applicability qualifiers; leaves details available for claim-specific structured extensions.

{
  "claim_id": "claim_1",
  "paper_id": "paper_123",
  "claim_text": "Psilocybin reduced anxiety symptoms in treatment-resistant adults.",
  "subject": { /* SemanticField — see A1.2.4 */ },
  "predicate": { /* SemanticField */ },
  "object": { /* SemanticField */ },
  "claim_kind": "result",
  "epistemic_status": "empirical",
  "support_origin": "own_results",
  "source_span_ids": ["span_44"],
  "context": {
    "population": { /* SemanticField */ }
  },
  "details": {},
  "extractor_confidence": 0.81
}

Field meanings: claim_kind = result | background | hypothesis | method | interpretation | causal | correlational | descriptive; epistemic_status = empirical | conjectural | theoretical | cited | inferred | mixed; support_origin = own_results | cited_results | background | methodological | speculative.

A1.2.6 EvidenceItem

A first-class evidence object that supports, qualifies, or contradicts a claim. Stores queryable support (not loose evidence sentences); separates the HOW, the WHAT, and the presentation format; uses context for evidence qualifiers (species, population, assay type, dose, comparator); uses details for type-specific structure; uses top-level ontology for classification of the evidence item as a whole.

{
  "evidence_id": "ev_1",
  "paper_id": "paper_123",
  "role": "supports",
  "summary_text": "Mean anxiety score decreased by 3.1 points (95% CI -5.4 to -0.8, p=0.01, n=184).",
  "evidence_method": { "value": "randomized_controlled_trial",
                       "entity_type": "evidence_method", "ontology": null },
  "outcome_type":    { "value": "clinical_outcome",
                       "entity_type": "outcome_type", "ontology": null },
  "presentation_type": { "value": "table",
                         "entity_type": "presentation_type", "ontology": null },
  "source_span_ids": ["span_45"],
  "context": {
    "population": { /* SemanticField */ },
    "species":    { /* SemanticField → NCBITaxon:9606 */ }
  },
  "details": {
    "outcome_name": "anxiety score",
    "effect_size":  -3.1,
    "unit":         "scale points",
    "ci_low":       -5.4,
    "ci_high":      -0.8,
    "p_value":      0.01,
    "sample_size":  184
  },
  "ontology": { /* OntologyAnnotation — top-level classification of the evidence item */ }
}

Field meanings: evidence_method — randomized_controlled_trial, laboratory_experiment, regression_estimate, observation, simulation, meta_analysis, theoretical_argument, mathematical_derivation. outcome_type — clinical_outcome, molecular_binding, gene_expression, physiological_measure, adverse_event. presentation_type — text, table, figure, caption, supplement. role — supports, contradicts, qualifies, backgrounds.

A1.2.7 ClaimEvidenceLink

The explicit relation between a claim and an evidence item. Keeps claim/evidence linkage explicit; allows one evidence item to relate to many claims and one claim to be backed by many evidence items.

{
  "link_id": "link_1",
  "claim_id": "claim_1",
  "evidence_id": "ev_1",
  "relation": "supports",
  "confidence": 0.92
}

A1.3 Profiles

Profiles define the expected shape of context and details without changing the core schema. The base object model stays fixed; profiles only say which keys are relevant for a given use case.

A1.3.1 Claim profile (recommended default)

{
  "allowed_context_keys": ["population", "species", "setting", "timepoint", "comparator"],
  "allowed_details_keys": []
}

Claims usually need only lightweight qualifiers; most heavy structured data lives in evidence.

A1.3.2 Evidence-method profiles

Randomized controlled trial:

{
  "evidence_method": "randomized_controlled_trial",
  "allowed_context_keys": ["population", "species", "setting", "dose", "timepoint", "comparator"],
  "allowed_details_keys": ["outcome_name", "effect_size", "unit", "ci_low", "ci_high", "p_value", "sample_size"]
}

Laboratory experiment:

{
  "evidence_method": "laboratory_experiment",
  "allowed_context_keys": ["species", "assay_type", "dose", "timepoint", "temperature", "ph"],
  "allowed_details_keys": ["measurement_type", "outcome_name", "value", "unit", "ci_low", "ci_high", "sample_size", "target"]
}

Meta-analysis:

{
  "evidence_method": "meta_analysis",
  "allowed_context_keys": ["population", "species", "setting"],
  "allowed_details_keys": ["outcome_name", "effect_size", "unit", "ci_low", "ci_high", "p_value", "study_count", "sample_size"]
}

A1.3.3 Outcome-type profiles

Examples: clinical_outcome — observed clinical endpoint, symptom score, or patient outcome. molecular_binding — binding affinity, receptor activation, or related molecular readout.

A1.3.4 Presentation-type profiles

Examples: table — evidence primarily presented in a table; text — evidence primarily described in narrative prose.

A1.4 Graph translation

The object model maps naturally to a property graph (e.g., Neo4j):

(Paper)-[:HAS_SPAN]->(Span)
(Paper)-[:HAS_CLAIM]->(Claim)
(Claim)-[:HAS_SUBJECT]->(Entity)
(Claim)-[:HAS_OBJECT]->(Entity)
(Claim)-[:SUPPORTED_BY]->(EvidenceItem)
(EvidenceItem)-[:ANCHORED_IN]->(Span)

Claims are nodes (not edges) because they carry metadata, provenance, and many evidence links. Subjects and objects can be stored as Entity nodes or flattened into claim fields in simpler prototypes. Most structured metadata can remain node properties rather than exploding into many small graph nodes. The V1 prototype26 exposes Cypher query examples that exercise this shape.

A1.5 Implementation contract for miners

Miners should be able to populate the schema in layers:

Minimum useful output: Paper, Span, Claim, EvidenceItem, ClaimEvidenceLink.

Better output: populate context and details; populate .ontology where confidence is high.

Best output: include candidate mappings when ontology is ambiguous; classify the evidence item ontology against ECO or a similar vocabulary; normalise predicates consistently.

A1.6 What this schema intentionally does not do

  • It does not force everything into flat SPO triples.
  • It does not require perfect ontology resolution before extraction is useful.
  • It does not force every evidence subtype into a new top-level object.
  • It does not require every structured field to become its own graph node.

Bottom line. Stable base objects; one reusable SemanticField pattern; one reusable OntologyAnnotation pattern; extensibility through context and details; graph shape built around Claim and EvidenceItem rather than raw triples. That gives the prototype a simple present-day structure without boxing it out of future ontology-aware querying.
